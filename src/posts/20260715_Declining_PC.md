title: declining my invitation to peace corps
tags: life
date: 2026-07-15

I received an offer from the Peace Corps to teach English for two years in Ecuador. I declined it.

This has been something I’ve been considering for years. Since high school, I’ve been dreaming of exploring the world and growing past my sheltered American bubble. My time in Taiwan has only strengthened this desire; there are so many places to see and experience, and so many different ways of living. I want to experience as many as I can, and let these experiences shape me. 

Peace Corps seemed a natural continuation of this path. Living in a new country would not only be an adventure, it would be an incredible opportunity to learn a new language, grow in unexpected ways, and make a difference in others’ lives. The chance to step away from the corporate rat race and slow down my life in a meaningful way is also deeply appealing to me. 

My grandparents have also been a major influence. After graduating university, they got married, flew across the country to Columbia University to learn Swahili, and then traveled to Kenya where they lived for seven years teaching English. They did this with a program called Teachers for East Africa, which pre-dated and acted as a proof of concept for the Peace Corps. (The two organizations eventually merged.) This kind of volunteer work, and the multilingualism of my grandparents (at her peak, my Nonna knew 13 languages!), has followed and inspired me all my life. 

I applied several months ago, for all these reasons. But over time, I realized it was the wrong decision for me, for now. Three things have held me back: the current, fluctuating state of the tech industry, mental health related misgivings during service, and organizational policies related to family emergencies.

For better or for worse, the software engineering world is in the midst of a pretty dramatic overhaul. The speed at which AI tooling is developing is increasingly altering the skills most in demand. Combined with an oversupply of CS graduates and years of tech layoffs, this means that the number of available entry and mid-level positions in CS has drastically decreased, and jobs that remain are infinitely more competitive. Although there would likely be ways I could serve my community by teaching technology as a side project, officially stepping away from the industry for two years at this time feels like career suicide.

More importantly, two things I’ve noticed from my time abroad that really help me to feel comfortable and safe in a new area are community and access to a body of water. My first few months in Taiwan were hard; the lack of connection to the existing community was compounded by the fact that, although I had studied Mandarin for years, I was not conversational in the language when I first arrived. This resulted in deep loneliness for several months, until daily exposure caught me up. Being on the ocean, and spending several hours a day on the beach or in the water, really helped me to regulate my mental health during that time. Knowing that I would have similar challenges in the Peace Corps, I selected Ecuador in large part because it had access to the coast. However, during my interview with the Peace Corps, I learned that not only would I very likely be the only Peace Corps volunteer in my town, but also the overwhelming majority of stations in Ecuador are in the mountains, hours from any major body of water. This meant that I would experience the same level of isolation and loneliness as I did in Taiwan, but without the ocean to help manage these emotions. 

Finally, the same people that left for Kenya at 22 are now in the late stages of their lives. Their health is declining, and I don’t have much time left with them. I learned during the interview process that grandparents don’t count as “immediate relatives” in the case of family emergencies, so if they get sick or end up passing, leave and transportation back to the US to see them or for their funeral are not guaranteed to be approved by the Peace Corps. This, combined with the other factors above, ended up being enough for me to decline the opportunity for the time being.

Turning down this offer sparks a pretty deep sense of sadness in me. Although I don’t think I would regret the life experience, the speed at which my industry is changing makes me incredibly nervous for how my life might proceed upon return. 

However, this dream is not dead for me. After extensive conversation with family and friends, I’ve narrowed down what really appeals to me about the Peace Corps: personal growth through exposure to disparate values and cultures across the globe, opportunities to challenge myself intellectually and existentially, and adventure. Sure, the Peace Corps Volunteer program offers a structured way to fulfill these needs, but other avenues exist that will allow me to pursue these goals while maintaining a stable career. For example, Peace Corps Response lets individuals apply their professional skills in order to make a larger impact on focused projects, albeit for a shorter period of time. Other examples include computer-science adjacent contract or freelance work, or a career pivot to teaching. For now, I’m allowing myself to slowly evaluate my options, while doing my best to keep in mind that life is long and fluid, and that I have time.
